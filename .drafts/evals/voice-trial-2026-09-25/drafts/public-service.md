A public service can be available and still be hard to reach. It may have a website, a phone number, and an office with posted hours. A person trying to use it still has to know which door to choose, what to call their problem, and which details to bring. For someone already stretched thin, the search itself can take the time they needed the service to save.

Imagine a person who knows something has to change but not which office handles it. They open a page organized around programs and eligibility terms they have never heard. Each choice seems to demand a decision: Is this me? Am I asking the right thing? If I choose badly, will I have to start again? They may close the page without finding out whether help was possible. The difficulty is built into where the process begins.

A more humane first step would let someone describe the situation in their own words. A person could explain what they are trying to do, hear which kinds of help might fit, and learn what information is needed before gathering it. The first answer should make the next one easier: who to contact, what to bring, and what will happen after that. If another office must take over, the person’s explanation should travel with them.

This would not remove every rule or make every request eligible. Some questions need time, and some services have limits. A useful beginning can still say so plainly. It can spare people from making a polished case before they know what the service can do.

The first question should leave a person more able to continue than before they asked it. Whether that takes a guide, a conversation, or a simpler form depends on the service. But the starting point can be human: tell us what is happening, and we will help you find the next step.
