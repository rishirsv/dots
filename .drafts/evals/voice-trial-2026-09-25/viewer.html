AI tools · 311 words

The application is still open

The application is still open

Someone wants to apply for a training program. The form is open on a laptop, with one question left: why do you want to attend? The answer is not a mystery. They want work that gives them more room to learn. They have said this to friends. In the box, the words keep sounding either too small or too grand.

An AI assistant can help here. It can ask what kind of work the person wants to do next, help turn an honest answer into a clear paragraph, find the deadline in the instructions, and put the remaining steps in order. The task that felt shapeless now has a next move. That matters. A good plan can make a hard beginning easier.

But “ready to submit” is not “submitted.” The assistant can produce a polished answer, a checklist, and a calendar reminder while the form stays open. It can leave the person with the satisfying sense that the work has been handled, though the part that puts the application forward—the actual submission—has not happened. The words are better. The application is still waiting.

An assistant should keep that line visible. It should say what it has done and what remains for the person to do. It can prepare an email and wait for permission to send it. If it submits a form at the person’s request, it should show the confirmation. A tidy sequence of steps is useful only as a path to the thing at the end.

Sometimes the final step will be a decision not to apply. That can count as progress if the person has weighed the cost and made the choice. An assistant cannot decide what counts as meaningful on someone else’s behalf. It can help them reach the moment when they can decide—and be honest about whether they’ve reached it.

Small business · 317 words

Before opening day

A bakery usually begins as a picture of a morning: the lights on before sunrise, a tray cooling by the window, somebody stopping in on the walk to work. Then the picture meets a lease, a budget, questions about what the space can be used for, and a list of rules the owner has to understand before turning on an oven. None of these questions is the bakery. They still have to be answered before it can exist.

The person who wants it can spend an evening comparing refrigerators and the next one trying to learn which office can answer a question about the space. One call leads to another. A number in a spreadsheet changes when equipment costs more than expected. A form asks for a decision they thought they could make later. The work comes in pieces, each small enough to seem manageable, and together they make opening day feel farther away than it did when the idea was only a room full of bread.

The plan can look like a matter of enthusiasm from the outside. But desire does not tell you which question to ask first, how to compare a lease, or what to do when an answer is unclear. Advice to “just start” can make an already crowded list feel like a personal failure.

A useful helper would make the next step visible. It could keep the questions in one place, turn a broad goal into a sequence, help draft the email to a landlord, and remember what answer is still missing. It could not decide whether the risk is worth taking, or promise the numbers will work. That choice belongs to the person who wants the bakery.

The first sign of progress might not smell like bread. It might be one clear answer, one cost checked, or a date written on the calendar with enough work behind it to mean something.

What this trial tests

The guide is drawn from one supplied sample. Its topic and emotional register may not transfer to every kind of writing.

01 / Sentence voice

Natural variation between short landings and longer gathering sentences, with plain spoken diction and emotional conviction.

02 / Human grounding

Specific moments and actions make the idea tangible without inventing the writer's own life.

03 / Earned movement

A recognizable need leads through an obstacle and consequence to a payoff that addresses that obstacle.

04 / Restraint

Original phrasing avoids copied sample language, repetitive rhetorical tricks, generic uplift, and promises it cannot earn.

05 / Claim discipline

Consequential claims are supported, qualified, or framed as possibilities; hypothetical scenes do not masquerade as reported fact.

Profile hypotheses

The source passage makes an ordinary human want feel large without abandoning concrete action. All five drafts use small scenes; most are less forceful at the larger turn.

The guide's caution about claims appears to have encouraged a measured, advisory tone. That may be right for practical writing and too restrained for a vision essay.

The subjects are exploratory. The source mentions family, social anxiety, food, building an app, and institutional obstacles, but it does not establish that the writer wants to publish on any of them.

Source passage

Read the passage used to build the guide

Most people never say what they want. It’s not because we don’t want. We want fiercely and constantly. It happens in the moments before falling asleep or while daydreaming on the car ride home. We want to spend more time with our families, overcome social anxiety, eat healthier food, open a bakery, build an app, or just find a few more moments of calm in a hectic life. But rarely do we get to even express these dreams, and even more rarely do they ever come true.

When we can’t name our dreams, we mostly can’t reach them. The world is a maze of forms and gatekeepers and schedules and obstacles, and most dreams die by a thousand paper cuts long before they are said aloud. They die in the not-knowing-where-to-start and in the phone calls never made.

Every unrealized want is a quiet tragedy of human potential.

Now imagine every person on earth with a second mind beyond their own. A general manager whose whole job is to find out what you want and make sure it happens. Someone that hears the half-sentence, “I feel like there’s something more I could be doing,” and helps fill in the blanks. This might not be a genie in a bottle, but it can be someone that says, “This is the thing you’ve been dreaming of your whole life. Let’s go get it. Here’s how.” And then builds a plan. Sends the email. Makes the phone call. Finds the funding. Keeps you on schedule. Clears the way and solves all the problems it can until the human part, the wanting and the dreaming, is all that’s left.

Here’s the best part: we have no idea what people will want. The world until now has been shaped by the small number of fanatics who have somehow found a way to make their wants real. But we’ve never seen humanity with every single person’s agency fully switched on. Eight billion people who just need to write their dream down in order to get started making it happen.
