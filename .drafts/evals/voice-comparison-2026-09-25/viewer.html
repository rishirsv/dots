AI tools / long form

The application is still open

Prompt An AI assistant that helps people finish one meaningful task, and the danger of mistaking a plan for progress. Write a 280–340 word original short essay. This is an exploratory trial, not a declaration of the writer's experiences or views. Avoid unsupported factual claims and do not copy phrases from the source sample.

V1 · original trial · 311 words

Someone wants to apply for a training program. The form is open on a laptop, with one question left: why do you want to attend? The answer is not a mystery. They want work that gives them more room to learn. They have said this to friends. In the box, the words keep sounding either too small or too grand.

An AI assistant can help here. It can ask what kind of work the person wants to do next, help turn an honest answer into a clear paragraph, find the deadline in the instructions, and put the remaining steps in order. The task that felt shapeless now has a next move. That matters. A good plan can make a hard beginning easier.

But “ready to submit” is not “submitted.” The assistant can produce a polished answer, a checklist, and a calendar reminder while the form stays open. It can leave the person with the satisfying sense that the work has been handled, though the part that puts the application forward—the actual submission—has not happened. The words are better. The application is still waiting.

An assistant should keep that line visible. It should say what it has done and what remains for the person to do. It can prepare an email and wait for permission to send it. If it submits a form at the person’s request, it should show the confirmation. A tidy sequence of steps is useful only as a path to the thing at the end.

Sometimes the final step will be a decision not to apply. That can count as progress if the person has weighed the cost and made the choice. An assistant cannot decide what counts as meaningful on someone else’s behalf. It can help them reach the moment when they can decide—and be honest about whether they’ve reached it.

V2 · Dan reference techniques · 330 words

The plan is not the work. Still, it can look enough like work to keep a person busy.

Imagine someone who wants to organize a neighborhood book swap. An assistant asks useful questions: where, when, what to bring, how to invite people. It turns answers into a tidy sequence, finds a date, drafts a note, makes a checklist. For a moment, the idea has shape. But if the note stays in a draft and no date reaches the calendar, the project has not moved. The assistant has reduced uncertainty; it has not crossed the small threshold where another person can say yes.

That distinction matters because planning is rewarding. Every question answered makes the next one easier to ask, and a fluent system can keep producing plausible next steps long after the useful ones are obvious. More structure may even protect the user from the awkward part: choosing a time, sending an invitation, asking for a room. Sometimes planning is how we postpone the point.

So the assistant should notice the handoff. Once there is enough information to act, it can offer a bounded next move: prepare the invitation, ask before sending it, or put the event on the calendar. If the choice has consequences or needs a human judgment, it should stop at the draft and make that decision clear. The aim is not to take over. It is to make the gap between intention and action visible, then help close the portion the person has chosen to delegate.

Of course, finishing one step does not guarantee a book swap, a full room, or even another reply. That is the honest limit. But it changes what can be learned. A sent invitation can be answered; a calendar hold can be accepted or declined. The plan becomes a test in the world instead of a polished place to wait.

A good assistant knows when another outline will add clarity—and when the next useful thing is already clear enough to do.

Small business / long form

Before opening day

Prompt A person wants to open a neighborhood bakery but keeps being stopped by the ordinary administrative and practical steps between desire and opening day. Write a 280–340 word original short essay. This is an exploratory trial, not a declaration of the writer's experiences or views. Avoid unsupported factual claims and do not copy phrases from the source sample.

V1 · original trial · 317 words

A bakery usually begins as a picture of a morning: the lights on before sunrise, a tray cooling by the window, somebody stopping in on the walk to work. Then the picture meets a lease, a budget, questions about what the space can be used for, and a list of rules the owner has to understand before turning on an oven. None of these questions is the bakery. They still have to be answered before it can exist.

The person who wants it can spend an evening comparing refrigerators and the next one trying to learn which office can answer a question about the space. One call leads to another. A number in a spreadsheet changes when equipment costs more than expected. A form asks for a decision they thought they could make later. The work comes in pieces, each small enough to seem manageable, and together they make opening day feel farther away than it did when the idea was only a room full of bread.

The plan can look like a matter of enthusiasm from the outside. But desire does not tell you which question to ask first, how to compare a lease, or what to do when an answer is unclear. Advice to “just start” can make an already crowded list feel like a personal failure.

A useful helper would make the next step visible. It could keep the questions in one place, turn a broad goal into a sequence, help draft the email to a landlord, and remember what answer is still missing. It could not decide whether the risk is worth taking, or promise the numbers will work. That choice belongs to the person who wants the bakery.

The first sign of progress might not smell like bread. It might be one clear answer, one cost checked, or a date written on the calendar with enough work behind it to mean something.

V2 · Dan reference techniques · 312 words

Picture someone who wants to open a bakery on their block. In the daydream, the place is already warm: bread on the counter, a neighbor at the door. Then the plan asks a colder question: what does it take to use a room for this?

The answer is a string of ordinary unknowns. What can the person afford? What kind of space might work? What should be checked before signing anything? Which forms apply, and who can explain them? Each question seems small until its answer changes the next decision. A lease estimate depends on the room; the room affects equipment; an application may ask for information the person hasn't gathered yet. The bakery remains easy to picture, but the picture is now tangled in a sequence.

It is tempting to treat every delay as useless bureaucracy. Some steps may be confusing or needlessly slow. Still, paperwork isn't automatically the enemy. A question about the room or budget can bring an expensive assumption into view early. If a process clarifies what must be ready before people use the space, that detail has a purpose. If nobody can explain what a form is for, that's a different problem. Calling both cases “red tape” makes it harder to know what should change.

Maybe the first goal is not to finish everything. It's to stop carrying every unknown at once. Put the unanswered questions on a page. Mark which decision each one affects, then ask the next person who can answer one of them. One call won't produce a storefront. It can get one answer, so the next decision rests on something more than a guess.

The bakery is still a tempting image: a warm room, bread on the counter. But now it includes an address to find, a budget to understand, and forms somebody can explain. Less effortless, maybe. More possible to work on.

Family time / long form

An hour on the calendar

Prompt Why wanting more time with family often fails to change a calendar, and what a small honest first step could be. Write a 280–340 word original short essay. This is an exploratory trial, not a declaration of the writer's experiences or views. Avoid unsupported factual claims and do not copy phrases from the source sample.

V1 · original trial · 284 words

A family can want more time together and still leave the calendar untouched. The agreement lives in a sentence—“We should do this more”—while the week is filled with named obligations. Work ends at six; someone needs a ride; groceries run out; a message arrives that seems to need an answer now. The wish has to compete with each thing separately, and no one has yet decided what can give.

Sometimes “more time” also means different things to different people. One person pictures dinner without phones; another wants an unhurried morning; a child may only want someone to watch the whole thing they are building on the floor. Without asking, a family may mistake shared affection for a shared plan. People may mean yes, then wait for someone else to choose what yes looks like.

A useful first step is a question at the table: “What’s one hour this week we’d like to keep for us?” Let each person answer. Pick a time that exists, put it on the calendar, and say what has to move for that hour to happen. If a shift, practice, chore, or tired evening gets in the way, say so plainly. The plan may need to change. Finding that out does not make the wish foolish.

An hour will not settle every pressure on a household, and it may not suit everyone. Afterward, the family can decide whether the hour felt worthwhile, what got in the way, and whether to try another time. A wish for more time is hard to plan around until someone asks what it means. One hour lets a family answer with a real evening, a real morning, or a reason the plan needs to wait.

V2 · Dan reference techniques · 289 words

Wanting more time with family can be sincere and still leave Tuesday untouched.

A calendar is a record of decisions already made, including the ones made by default. Work takes the afternoon. The grocery run follows pickup. A child begins a story just as someone opens a laptop again. In a hypothetical week like this, no single choice announces itself as a vote against family. Each has an explanation. But the week still shows where the available hours went. A wish has to become specific before it can compete with a meeting, a shift, or the errand that cannot wait.

That can sound like a simple scheduling problem, until the obligations are not optional. A shift may be fixed; care and bills are real. “Just make time” turns a crowded life into a character flaw. The better question is smaller and harder: what, exactly, would have to give? Maybe one evening chore can move. Maybe work owns every open hour this week, and the honest answer is that nothing can move yet.

One first step is to name a particular stretch of time and the tradeoff it requires: “Could we keep Thursday after dinner free? I’d need to shop Wednesday, and I can’t promise every Thursday.”

They might say Thursday is impossible, suggest another night, or explain that what they miss is a call, a shared meal, or being listened to without a phone between them. That answer could change the plan. A free hour matters only if it resembles the time together they had in mind.

Some weeks may have no hour to offer. Say so, and name when to revisit the question. If Thursday can work, put it on the calendar along with the errand that moved.

Social connection / long form

A smaller invitation

Prompt Social anxiety can make an ordinary invitation feel impossibly large; explore one practical way a person might make the first move without promising a cure. Write a 280–340 word original short essay. This is an exploratory trial, not a declaration of the writer's experiences or views. Avoid unsupported factual claims and do not copy phrases from the source sample.

V1 · original trial · 313 words

An invitation can be one sentence on a phone and still occupy the rest of the evening. “A few of us are getting dinner Friday. Want to come?” For someone who experiences social anxiety, the reply may open a dozen questions the sender never typed: Who will be there? How long will dinner last? What if conversation runs dry? How do I leave? The empty reply box sits there while each question grows another branch.

One way to make a first move is to offer a smaller plan with clear edges. Instead of deciding yes or no to the whole evening, a person could answer: “Thanks for asking. A group dinner feels like a lot right now. Would you be up for coffee for half an hour next week?” It answers the invitation, states a limit without a long defense, and leaves a door open to one kind of company that may feel more possible.

That sentence cannot promise that coffee will feel easy. The meeting could still be tense. The friend may be busy, and a no could hurt. Even sending the reply may take more effort than the words suggest. A practical step does not have to settle those feelings first. It can make one choice clearer while the feelings are still there.

The aim is not to make every social plan small or controlled. Some people want the dinner; some days a quiet evening is the right answer. But when a broad invitation feels too large to answer, a specific counteroffer gives the other person something they can say yes or no to. It also gives the person who received the invitation a way to move toward connection at a size they chose. The first move may be no more dramatic than suggesting coffee. Its limit is part of its use: it tests one plan, not whether anxiety is gone.

V2 · Dan reference techniques · 308 words

Picture the text: “Dinner Friday?” For someone who wants company and feels anxious about being there, the invitation can arrive carrying several decisions at once: what to say, how long to stay, what to do if the room goes quiet, when it is okay to leave. A simple yes can feel like signing up for the whole unknown evening. A simple no may close a door the person wanted open.

That is a real bind, but it may not be the only available choice. The first move can be a counteroffer small enough to picture: “Dinner feels like a lot this week. Would you take a short walk with me Saturday?” There is a limit in that sentence, and there is an offer. The person is not asked to become confident before reaching out; they are choosing a form of contact they can imagine trying.

It helps that the alternative is concrete. “We should hang out sometime” leaves the next step floating. A time and a modest plan give both people a question they can answer: Saturday or another day? A walk or coffee? A counteroffer also makes room for the friend to say no, or to suggest something else. It cannot guarantee an easy exchange.

That boundary matters. One text will not erase anxiety, and even a smaller plan may still feel hard. A counteroffer cannot promise a cure. It can offer one possible way to make the first choice less all-or-nothing. If the person does not want to send it, they do not owe the message to anyone.

Maybe the useful measure of the first move is not whether fear disappears. It is whether the person can say, honestly, “I want to see you—and this is a way I might manage it.” The fear can come along. The plan can still be small enough to try.

Public services / long form

The first door

Prompt A public service can be technically available yet too difficult to reach; explore what a more humane starting point might look like. Write a 280–340 word original short essay. This is an exploratory trial, not a declaration of the writer's experiences or views. Avoid unsupported factual claims and do not copy phrases from the source sample.

V1 · original trial · 316 words

A public service can be available and still be hard to reach. It may have a website, a phone number, and an office with posted hours. A person trying to use it still has to know which door to choose, what to call their problem, and which details to bring. For someone already stretched thin, the search itself can take the time they needed the service to save.

Imagine a person who knows something has to change but not which office handles it. They open a page organized around programs and eligibility terms they have never heard. Each choice seems to demand a decision: Is this me? Am I asking the right thing? If I choose badly, will I have to start again? They may close the page without finding out whether help was possible. The difficulty is built into where the process begins.

A more humane first step would let someone describe the situation in their own words. A person could explain what they are trying to do, hear which kinds of help might fit, and learn what information is needed before gathering it. The first answer should make the next one easier: who to contact, what to bring, and what will happen after that. If another office must take over, the person’s explanation should travel with them.

This would not remove every rule or make every request eligible. Some questions need time, and some services have limits. A useful beginning can still say so plainly. It can spare people from making a polished case before they know what the service can do.

The first question should leave a person more able to continue than before they asked it. Whether that takes a guide, a conversation, or a simpler form depends on the service. But the starting point can be human: tell us what is happening, and we will help you find the next step.

V2 · Dan reference techniques · 292 words

A public service can be available and still begin one step too late.

Imagine someone arriving at an office with a letter they don't understand. The first question is, “Which program are you asking about?” But the name of the program is exactly what they don't know. They came with a problem; now they must translate it into the institution's categories before the conversation can start.

A different first question—“What are you trying to get done?”—doesn't promise a solution. It gives the person a place to begin. Maybe they need a document replaced, an appointment moved, or an answer about a notice. The service can then connect that need to its own programs and rules. The categories don't disappear. They stop being the price of entry.

That small change carries a larger idea about access. A service is not fully reachable just because its office is open or its website is online. The starting point matters too. If the first move assumes a visitor already understands the system, the institution asks them to do the translation before it can offer help.

There are real limits. A better opening cannot make a scarce appointment appear, erase eligibility rules, or answer every request with yes. It can make a no understandable and show what may happen next. It may also surface needs a menu of program names would miss.

The goal is not to make public services sound friendlier while leaving people to navigate alone. It is to let the institution meet someone in the words they actually have, then carry the conversation toward the right next step. The first question will not fix the whole service. But it can decide whether the service begins with the need or with its own filing system.

V2 voice · V2 style

Rubric

Long-form dimensions: sentence voice, human grounding, earned movement, restraint, and claim discipline, each scored from 1 to 5. Short-form voice fit is scored from 1 to 5 for clarity, specificity, and a turn that feels earned. No score can establish that a draft truly sounds like you.
