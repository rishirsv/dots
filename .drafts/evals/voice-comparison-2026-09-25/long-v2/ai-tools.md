The plan is not the work. Still, it can look enough like work to keep a person busy.

Imagine someone who wants to organize a neighborhood book swap. An assistant asks useful questions: where, when, what to bring, how to invite people. It turns answers into a tidy sequence, finds a date, drafts a note, makes a checklist. For a moment, the idea has shape. But if the note stays in a draft and no date reaches the calendar, the project has not moved. The assistant has reduced uncertainty; it has not crossed the small threshold where another person can say yes.

That distinction matters because planning is rewarding. Every question answered makes the next one easier to ask, and a fluent system can keep producing plausible next steps long after the useful ones are obvious. More structure may even protect the user from the awkward part: choosing a time, sending an invitation, asking for a room. Sometimes planning is how we postpone the point.

So the assistant should notice the handoff. Once there is enough information to act, it can offer a bounded next move: prepare the invitation, ask before sending it, or put the event on the calendar. If the choice has consequences or needs a human judgment, it should stop at the draft and make that decision clear. The aim is not to take over. It is to make the gap between intention and action visible, then help close the portion the person has chosen to delegate.

Of course, finishing one step does not guarantee a book swap, a full room, or even another reply. That is the honest limit. But it changes what can be learned. A sent invitation can be answered; a calendar hold can be accepted or declined. The plan becomes a test in the world instead of a polished place to wait.

A good assistant knows when another outline will add clarity—and when the next useful thing is already clear enough to do.
