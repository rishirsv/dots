Picture someone who wants to open a bakery on their block. In the daydream, the place is already warm: bread on the counter, a neighbor at the door. Then the plan asks a colder question: what does it take to use a room for this?

The answer is a string of ordinary unknowns. What can the person afford? What kind of space might work? What should be checked before signing anything? Which forms apply, and who can explain them? Each question seems small until its answer changes the next decision. A lease estimate depends on the room; the room affects equipment; an application may ask for information the person hasn't gathered yet. The bakery remains easy to picture, but the picture is now tangled in a sequence.

It is tempting to treat every delay as useless bureaucracy. Some steps may be confusing or needlessly slow. Still, paperwork isn't automatically the enemy. A question about the room or budget can bring an expensive assumption into view early. If a process clarifies what must be ready before people use the space, that detail has a purpose. If nobody can explain what a form is for, that's a different problem. Calling both cases “red tape” makes it harder to know what should change.

Maybe the first goal is not to finish everything. It's to stop carrying every unknown at once. Put the unanswered questions on a page. Mark which decision each one affects, then ask the next person who can answer one of them. One call won't produce a storefront. It can get one answer, so the next decision rests on something more than a guess.

The bakery is still a tempting image: a warm room, bread on the counter. But now it includes an address to find, a budget to understand, and forms somebody can explain. Less effortless, maybe. More possible to work on.
