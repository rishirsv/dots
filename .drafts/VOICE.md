# Voice guide — provisional

This is a first pass based on one user-supplied passage about human wants and agency (saved in `examples/agency-passage.md`). It describes repeatable sentence behavior observed there, not a fixed persona. Assignment-specific instructions can override it. Revisit after feedback on the trial pieces.

## Core sound

Write in clear, spoken English with emotional conviction. Let ordinary situations carry an ambitious idea. The passage moves from intimate moments to a sweeping possibility without switching to academic or corporate language.

## Sentence rhythm and construction

- Open a thought with a plain, declarative claim, then qualify or deepen it. The sample begins with a short assertion and immediately explains the tension behind it.
- Mix brief sentences that land a point with longer sentences that gather examples or consequences. Save the shortest line for a real turn; do not make every paragraph a sequence of punch lines.
- Use a correction when it sharpens meaning: “It’s not because we don’t want. We want fiercely and constantly.” Use this move sparingly so it still feels discovered.
- Let lists move from recognizable daily wants toward more unusual or consequential ones. The sample ranges from family time and food to a bakery and an app.
- Prefer direct transitions that advance the thought. A paragraph can begin with the consequence, a new image, or an invitation to imagine.

## Diction and tone

- Favor familiar words and concrete human actions: wanting, calling, writing, finding, building, waiting. Bring abstractions down to moments a reader can picture.
- Keep a humane, earnest tone. Make room for frustration and difficulty without sounding cynical or scolding.
- Use “we” for a shared condition and “you” when placing the reader inside a possible scene. Do not claim to know every reader's experience.
- A strong metaphor can make friction visible; the sample uses a maze and small obstacles. Avoid stacking metaphors or turning every sentence into a slogan.
- Avoid corporate optimization language, generic uplift, and inflated descriptions that have no concrete action beneath them.

## Observed moves, not reusable phrases

| Move | What it does | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Small moment to large claim | Grounds an abstract idea in recognizable experience | Claim outruns the evidence |
| Accumulating list | Gives a broad idea human variety and momentum | Examples feel random or repetitive |
| Short moral sentence | Lets a hard consequence land | Sounds melodramatic when the prior paragraph has not earned it |
| Imagined future scene | Turns a concept into something a person could say or do | Becomes a product pitch or promises too much |

## Revision check

Read aloud for a natural shift between long and short sentences. Replace vague uplift with a person, action, or obstacle. Keep the emotional point, but remove a sweeping claim if the piece cannot support it. Do not borrow distinctive phrases from the sample; use its moves to express the new subject.
