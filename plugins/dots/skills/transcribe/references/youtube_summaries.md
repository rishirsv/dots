# YouTube summaries

Read this after producing and inspecting a YouTube transcript when the user
wants a summary, notes, takeaways, an explanation, or an action plan. The
transcript is the source; do not browse for extra context unless the user asks
for research or verification.

## Let the task set the depth

Choose the minimum depth that fully serves what the reader will do next. An
explicit request for brevity, detail, study notes, or exhaustive coverage
controls. Otherwise infer depth from the reader's decision, the video's
information density, and the cost of missing nuance.

- **Glance:** Use for orientation or a narrow question. Lead with the direct
  answer or thesis, then only the few points needed to understand it.
- **Working:** Use by default for a general summary. Give the thesis, grouped
  ideas, the strongest examples or reasoning, and practical implications.
- **Deep:** Use for learning, consequential decisions, dense technical
  material, or a request for detailed notes. Preserve the argument or process,
  evidence and examples, caveats, and connections between sections.
- **Reference:** Use when the result must substitute for watching the video.
  Build a navigable chapter map with comprehensive notes, important details,
  and timestamps that make each section recoverable.

Increase depth when the video introduces several dependent ideas, makes claims
whose support matters, teaches a process, or will guide action. Decrease it
when the user's question is narrow, the material is repetitive, or a quick
orientation is enough. A long video does not automatically need a long
summary; density and use matter more than duration.

## Shape the summary around the job

Do not force every summary into the same headings. Compose only the parts that
help with the current task:

- For a **question**, answer it first, give the supporting reasoning, and link
  the few timestamps that let the reader verify or continue.
- For **learning**, organize concepts by dependency; include definitions,
  examples, misconceptions, and a compact mental model.
- For a **decision or analysis**, separate the speaker's claims, supporting
  evidence or examples, assumptions, counterpoints, and implications.
- For a **tutorial or action request**, preserve prerequisites, ordered steps,
  decision points, warnings, and a usable next-action checklist.
- For an **interview, meeting, or conversation**, group themes and attribute
  views only when the transcript identifies speakers reliably; surface
  decisions, disagreements, commitments, and open questions when present.
- For a **narrative or commentary**, preserve the central arc, turning points,
  illustrative stories, and conclusion without retelling every beat.

Use timestamps at major claims, transitions, demonstrations, and action steps.
Do not timestamp every bullet when a section-level timestamp is enough.

## Preserve fidelity while compressing

- Treat a claim made in the video as the speaker's claim, not an established
  fact. Add outside verification only when the user asks for it.
- Separate **what the video says** from **suggested application**. When the
  user's task benefits from a checklist, schedule, exercise, or recommendation
  that the video did not provide, label it as an adaptation and do not
  attribute its details to the speaker.
- Paraphrase by default. Use quotation marks only for wording checked against
  the transcript, and remember that automatic captions may be wrong.
- Remove sponsor copy, housekeeping, repeated framing, music markers, and
  verbal filler unless they affect the user's task.
- Preserve uncertainty, qualifications, counterexamples, and unresolved
  questions that change the conclusion.
- Do not invent speaker names, chapter boundaries, evidence, or causal links.
  If transcript quality makes an important point unclear, say what is unclear
  and point to its timestamp.

## Adjust after the first draft

Read the draft against the user's task and the transcript:

1. Expand any conclusion whose reasoning, caveat, or next step is missing.
2. Compress repeated points and sections that do not change understanding or
   action.
3. Check that the opening gives the answer or thesis at the selected depth.
4. Check that source-derived points and agent-designed application are visibly
   distinct.
5. Check that important statements remain traceable to useful timestamps.
6. Stop when another layer of detail would add retrieval material rather than
   improve the reader's decision, learning, or action.

The summary is complete when it answers the user's actual task, preserves the
video's material reasoning at the chosen depth, and can be used without first
reading the full transcript.
